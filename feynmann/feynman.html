Who was Richard Feynman?

The physicist this app is named after, and the idea we borrowed from him.

Richard Feynman (1918–1988) was an American physicist and one of the great teachers of the twentieth century. He won a Nobel Prize, played the bongo drums, cracked safes for fun, and had a lifelong habit of refusing to accept an explanation he couldn't rebuild for himself.

The physicist

He grew up in Queens, New York, and worked on the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos while still in his twenties. In 1965 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga for their work on quantum electrodynamics, the theory of how light and matter interact. The simple sketches he invented to keep track of it, now called Feynman diagrams, are still drawn by physicists everywhere.

The teacher

At Caltech he taught first-year physics himself. Those classes became The Feynman Lectures on Physics, still read sixty years later because they explain hard ideas the way you'd explain them to a curious friend: from the ground up, in plain words.

In 1986, investigating the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster, he made the cause plain to a room full of experts and TV cameras. He squeezed a piece of the shuttle's rubber seal into a C-clamp, dropped it into his glass of ice water, and showed that, cold, it didn't spring back. No jargon needed.

The idea we borrowed

When he died, this was written on his blackboard at Caltech:

“What I cannot create, I do not understand.”
Richard Feynman’s blackboard, 1988

Out of that attitude grew what's now called the Feynman technique:

  1. Pick something you want to understand.
  2. Explain it in plain words, as if to someone new to it.
  3. Notice where you get stuck or reach for jargon. That's the gap.
  4. Go back, fill the gap, and explain it again, simpler.

Why “your own Feynmann”

Most of us never had a teacher like that: one who starts from what we already know, takes one small step at a time, and keeps asking “but what if…?” until we really get it. So we built one, for anything you're curious about. You learn an idea, then teach it back. Your Feynmann pokes holes until there are none left.

(The extra “n” is on purpose: Feynmann is the tutor, Richard Feynman is the man. Feynmann is an independent project and isn't affiliated with Feynman's family or estate.)
